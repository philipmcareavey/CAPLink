What actually counts as evidence of graduate outcomes?

By · · 6 min read · ← Back to blog

Every careers service is asked, in one form or another, to show that what it does actually leads somewhere. The honest difficulty is that most of what a service can measure — footfall at events, click-throughs on a job board, survey responses about "confidence" — is activity, not outcome. None of it tells you whether a student actually did any paid, relevant work before they graduated.

Activity isn't outcome

A student attending a CV workshop is a genuinely useful thing to know. It's also not evidence that the workshop led to anything. The same is true of listings-board traffic: knowing how many students viewed a vacancy tells you nothing about how many were hired, what they were paid, or whether the work was relevant to their course.

Outcome reporting has to be built from things that actually happened, not things that were merely offered. A completed, paid piece of work — with a real business, a real agreed scope, and a real payment released on completion — is a fundamentally different kind of evidence than a listing being viewed.

What CAPLink actually reports

Because every project on CAPLink runs through the platform end to end — the brief, the application, the agreed milestones, the payment — the reporting a careers team gets isn't self-reported. It reflects what genuinely happened: how many of your students applied to something, how many were hired, how many actually completed paid work, what they earned doing it, and how that breaks down by year group.

  • Applied, hired and completed counts, specific to your own institution's students.
  • Total student earnings from completed work.
  • A breakdown by year group/band, so a foundation-year cohort's outcomes aren't averaged away by your final-year students'.

None of this replaces a graduate outcomes survey, and it isn't meant to. It's a narrower, harder-edged data point that sits alongside your existing evidence: not "students say they feel more employable," but "this many students did this much real, paid work, for this many local businesses."

Why that distinction matters for reporting

When a careers service has to account for its own impact — to a faculty, to leadership, or as part of a wider accountability framework — activity-based measures are easy to produce but easy to dismiss. Outcome-based measures, built from records of things that genuinely happened rather than things that were offered, are harder to produce and much harder to argue with.
